\begin{abstract}
\noindent We take the role of a government agency that checks whether pay is fair with respect to sex and race, under the principle of \emph{equal pay for equal work}. We train a \emph{reference pay model} that predicts the probability of earning more than \$50K from job-related factors only, and never sees sex, race, national origin or their proxies. The audit then compares, for each group, the actual share of high earners with the share expected from its qualifications. The final model is a tuned LightGBM with isotonic calibration (test ROC-AUC 0.893, average precision 0.777, expected calibration error 0.009). On the untouched test set, women earn more than \$50K \textbf{5.4 points less often} than their profiles predict (95\% CI $-6.3$ to $-4.6$), and Black employees \textbf{3.4 points less often} ($-4.9$ to $-1.9$).

\noindent A model trained on all columns is more accurate but hides these gaps entirely, which is why removing protected attributes is the right mitigation for this use case.
\end{abstract}

% ======================================================================
\section{Problem framing}
% ======================================================================

\paragraph{Business question:} A pay-equity regulator must decide which employers to investigate. Comparing the raw share of high earners between men and women is not enough: groups may differ in education, occupation or working time, which legitimately affect pay. The question is therefore: \emph{do people with the same job-related profile have the same chance of earning more than \$50K, whatever their sex or race?}

\paragraph{Approach:} We learn $p(\text{income}>50\text{K} \mid \text{job-related factors})$ on the population. For a group $g$ of size $n_g$, with actual labels $y_i$ and calibrated probabilities $\hat p_i$, we define
\begin{equation}
\text{unexplained gap}_g \;=\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{n_g}\textstyle\sum_{i\in g} y_i}_{\text{actual rate}} \;-\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{n_g}\textstyle\sum_{i\in g} \hat p_i}_{\text{expected rate}} .
\end{equation}
A significantly negative gap means the group earns less than its qualifications predict: a reason to open an investigation, \emph{not} a proof of discrimination. This framing has three methodological consequences that structure the whole project:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Protected attributes and their proxies must not be inputs}, otherwise the model learns the discrimination and the expected rate already contains it.
\item \textbf{Probabilities must be calibrated}, because the expected rate is an average of probabilities: inflated probabilities would make every group look underpaid.
\item \textbf{Ranking quality matters} (ROC-AUC, average precision), because the expected rate must follow the real differences in qualifications.
\end{enumerate}
Individual employees whose profile makes $>$\$50K more likely than not but who earn less are additionally flagged as \emph{potentially underpaid}, for inspectors to review.

\section{Data and exploratory analysis}
% ======================================================================

\paragraph{Overview:} The 1994 US census extract contains 48{,}842 people, 6 numeric and 8 categorical columns. 23.9\% earn more than \$50K: the classes are imbalanced, and a model that always predicts ``$\le$50K'' reaches 76.1\% accuracy with zero recall, so accuracy is not a useful metric here. Missing values concern only \texttt{workclass} (5.7\%), \texttt{occupation} (5.8\%, almost always together with \texttt{workclass}) and \texttt{native-country} (1.8\%). Missingness is informative: only 9.5\% of people with an unknown occupation earn $>$50K, against 24.8\% otherwise. We therefore encode it as an explicit \texttt{"Missing"} category instead of imputing the most frequent value. \texttt{capital-gain} and \texttt{capital-loss} are zero for 91.7\% and 95.3\% of people.

\paragraph{Sensitive attributes:} The raw gaps are large: 30.4\% of men earn $>$50K against 10.9\% of women; 25.4\% of White people against 12.1\% of Black people. Table~\ref{tab:gap_within} shows that the sex gap \emph{remains between people with the same education or the same occupation}: legitimate factors do not explain it, which motivates the audit.

\paragraph{Leakage audit:} Every kept column is known at prediction time, with one caveat: the target is \emph{total} income, which includes capital gains. \texttt{capital-gain} is therefore a partial target leak. We keep it, because ignoring investment income would attribute it to the employer, and we verify in Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} that the conclusions do not depend on it.

\paragraph{Split:} We use a stratified 60/20/20 split (\texttt{random\_state=42}): 29{,}304 training, 9{,}769 validation and 9{,}769 test people, each with a 23.9\% positive rate. The training set is used for fitting and cross-validation, the validation set for model choice, calibration choice and threshold, and the test set is opened \textbf{once}, at the end, for the final evaluation and the audit.

\section{Methodology}
% ======================================================================

\subsection{Feature engineering and pipeline}

A custom scikit-learn transformer, \texttt{AdultFeatureEngineer}, drops the excluded columns and adds five features (Table~\ref{tab:features}). Numeric columns are then imputed with the median and standardised; categorical columns are imputed with \texttt{"Missing"} and one-hot encoded, with rare categories ($<$0.5\%) grouped together and unknown categories handled at prediction time. All steps and the model are wrapped in a single \texttt{Pipeline}, so every preprocessing step is fitted on the training data (or the training folds) only, and the excluded columns can never reach the model, even at serving time.

\centering\small
\caption{Engineered features and their signal on the training set.}
\label{tab:features}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{llX}
\toprule
\rowcolor{headblue}
Feature & Definition & Justification and signal ($>$50K rate) \\
\midrule
\texttt{capital\_net} & gain $-$ loss & net investment result in one number \\

The working-time features matter for the audit: women work part-time much more often, a legitimate reason for lower annual income that the model must account for.

\subsection{Metrics and class imbalance}

We report precision, recall and F1 at the decision threshold, and two threshold-free ranking metrics: ROC-AUC and \textbf{average precision} (AP, the area under the precision--recall curve, called \texttt{pr\_auc} in our code; a random model scores the prevalence, 0.24). AP is our main selection metric, since it focuses on the rare class.

On the Logistic Regression baseline, we compared three remedies (Table~\ref{tab:imbalance}). All three give the same trade-off: recall rises from 0.43 to about 0.76, precision falls from 0.74 to about 0.51, F1 improves, and ROC-AUC and AP do not move. The remedies shift the operating point (they are close to lowering the threshold), they do not improve the ranking. We keep \textbf{class weights} (\texttt{class\_weight="balanced"}): same result, all real data is kept (undersampling discards two thirds of the majority class), no synthetic people (SMOTE interpolates one-hot columns into unrealistic profiles), and the same parameter exists for all three models. Resampling, when used, is applied inside an \texttt{imblearn} pipeline, during \texttt{fit} only.

\noindent We treat the class weight as fixed rather than as a hyperparameter: it did not change the ranking, and the precision/recall trade-off is controlled later by the threshold. Its side effect on the probabilities is corrected by calibration (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}).

\subsection{Models, tuning and experiment tracking}

We compare Logistic Regression (linear, interpretable), Random Forest (bagged deep trees, captures interactions) and LightGBM (gradient boosting), all with class weights and the same pipeline. Hyperparameters are tuned with \textbf{Optuna} (TPE sampler, seed 42): 15 trials for Logistic Regression and the Random Forest, 30 for LightGBM. Each trial is scored by \textbf{5-fold stratified cross-validation on the training set} (\texttt{scoring="average\_precision"}), refitting the whole pipeline in each fold; the validation set is never used during the search, so the selection does not overfit it. Search spaces cover the complexity parameters of each model (Appendix~\ref{app:spaces}).

Every run is logged in \textbf{MLflow} (experiment \texttt{adult-pay-equity}, SQLite backend): parameters, metrics, confusion matrices and fitted pipelines for the imbalance and model comparisons; one parent run per model with one nested child run per Optuna trial (parameters, CV mean and standard deviation, the five fold scores, run time) and the optimisation history; and a final run with the test metrics, the audit tables and the calibrated model. Runs are tagged by stage (\texttt{imbalance}, \texttt{models}, \texttt{tuning}, \texttt{tuned}, \texttt{final\_test}).

\paragraph{What each step bought:} Moving from Logistic Regression to the Random Forest brings the largest gain (AP $+0.06$, ROC-AUC $+0.025$): trees capture some interactions that a linear score cannot, for example the return on education depends on occupation and age. LightGBM adds a further gain (AP $+0.02$) with the best recall, and trains in about 1\,s against 5\,s for the forest. Both tree models overfit mildly (train vs validation ROC-AUC: 0.916 vs 0.879 for the forest, 0.911 vs 0.890 for LightGBM); Logistic Regression does not.

\paragraph{What tuning bought:} Very little: AP rises by $+0.005$ for LightGBM, $+0.001$ for the forest and $\approx0$ for Logistic Regression on validation, which is smaller than the cross-validation standard deviation. Tuning mostly reduced overfitting: training AP drops from 0.802 to 0.788 for LightGBM and from 0.807 to 0.783 for the forest while validation AP slightly rises. The best LightGBM uses many small steps (600 trees, learning rate 0.023) and moderate trees (22 leaves, 51\% of features per tree). The CV and validation rankings agree, and validation scores are about 0.01 \emph{above} the CV scores for every model (tuned or not), so the selection over many trials did not produce an optimistic estimate. \textbf{The model family mattered more than the hyperparameters.}

\paragraph{Final choice:} We select the tuned \textbf{LightGBM}: best validation AP and ROC-AUC, consistent CV and validation results, fast training, and exact, fast SHAP explanations for trees.

\subsection{Explainability}

We explain the final model with SHAP (\texttt{TreeExplainer}, log-odds scale) on 2{,}000 validation people. Globally (Figure~\ref{fig:shap}a), the most important features are \textbf{age} (mean $|$SHAP$|$ 1.09), \textbf{education} (0.47), \textbf{capital gain} and \texttt{capital\_net} (0.29, 0.22), \textbf{hours per week} (0.20) and \textbf{occupations} (\texttt{Other-service}, \texttt{Exec-managerial}, \texttt{Adm-clerical}). Four of the five engineered features are in the top 15. These are exactly the factors that pay should legitimately depend on: experience, qualification, working time, investment income and type of job. The directions make sense: older, more educated people working longer hours are pushed towards $>$50K; service and clerical occupations push down. SHAP describes what the model uses, not what causes income.

Locally, the instructive cases are the model's mistakes (Figure~\ref{fig:shap}b and Appendix~\ref{app:figures}). The ``missed high earner'' is a 46-year-old woman with an 11th-grade education working 35 hours a week in \texttt{Other-service}: every job-related factor points to $\le$50K, her high income is genuinely unusual for her profile. The ``false alarm'' is a self-employed 50-year-old with a professional degree whom everything predicts above \$50K: exactly what a ``potentially underpaid'' flag looks like, but with no employer to blame, which shows that flags are starting points for an inspector, not conclusions.

The raw model is \textbf{over-confident towards $>$50K}: it predicts 37\% on average while the true rate is 24\%, and its reliability curve lies below the diagonal (Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}a). This is the expected side effect of class weights, which make each positive count about three times more during training. \textbf{We would not trust these probabilities as-is}: the audit would declare almost every group underpaid. We recalibrate with \texttt{CalibratedClassifierCV(cv=5)}, which learns the mapping on out-of-fold predictions of the training set, so neither the validation nor the test set is used. Isotonic regression clearly beats the sigmoid (ECE three times lower), and with 29{,}000 training people it has enough data not to overfit, so we keep it; AP is unchanged since calibration preserves the ranking. Bin counts (Appendix~\ref{app:figures}) show that the low-probability region, which drives the group averages, is estimated on thousands of people, while bins between 0.5 and 0.9 contain only 90 to 460 people; there the model is, if anything, slightly cautious (observed 0.75 for 0.65 predicted).

\subsection{Decision rule and final test}

Individual flags use the calibrated probability with a threshold of \textbf{0.5} (``more likely than not''). Since a flag may trigger an inspection and inspectors are limited, precision matters more than recall: at 0.5, 82\% of people expected to earn $>$50K really do (recall 0.51), whereas the F1-optimal threshold (0.39) would trade precision down to 0.68 for a recall of 0.65. The group audit does not depend on the threshold, as it uses the probabilities directly.

Unexplained gap, Black employees & $+$1.1\pts & $-$3.4\pts \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent The mitigation costs predictive performance (ROC-AUC $-0.04$, F1 $-0.10$) but almost halves the demographic-parity gap and nearly divides the equalized-odds gap by three. Above all, it changes the audit: the ``before'' model is more accurate \emph{because it has learned the discrimination}. It expects lower pay for women and Black employees, finds them paid exactly as expected, and would declare every employer fair. A reference model must describe what people \emph{should} earn for their work, not what they \emph{do} earn given their sex and race.

We rejected \textbf{post-processing} (e.g.\ fairlearn's \texttt{ThresholdOptimizer}). It equalises error rates with group-specific thresholds, which suits a model that \emph{decides} about people (hiring, credit), but here the extra ``errors'' for women are the underpayment signal: equalising them would erase the evidence, and it would require sex and race at prediction time.

\subsection{Robustness checks}\label{sec:sensitivity}

\paragraph{Proxy check:} A classifier predicting sex from the model's inputs reaches ROC-AUC 0.79, mainly through occupation and hours. This is intended under ``equal pay for equal work'' (part-time work legitimately lowers annual income), but it means the gap only measures unequal pay \emph{for comparable jobs}: discrimination in access to well-paid occupations is ``explained'' by occupation and remains invisible. Our gaps are therefore a \textbf{lower bound}.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to the capital columns:} Retraining without \texttt{capital-gain}, \texttt{capital-loss} and their engineered features lowers ROC-AUC from 0.893 to 0.846, but the conclusions hold and the gaps become slightly larger: $-6.6$\pts for women (CI $-7.6$ to $-5.5$) and $-3.8$\pts for Black employees ($-5.6$ to $-2.0$), because investment income is more frequent among men. Keeping these columns is the conservative choice for employers.

\section{Serving}
% ======================================================================

The calibrated pipeline is exported with \texttt{cloudpickle} together with its settings, and served by a \textbf{FastAPI} application (\texttt{serving/audit\_api.py}) with Pydantic input validation. \texttt{GET /health} checks the service; \texttt{POST /predict} returns each employee's expected probability; \texttt{POST /audit} takes a company's employee records and returns, per sex and race group, the actual and expected rates, the gap with its bootstrap CI, and the potentially underpaid employees. Tests with FastAPI's \texttt{TestClient} confirm that two employees with the same job profile but different sex and race receive exactly the same probability (0.652), and that invalid inputs (e.g.\ age 7) are rejected with HTTP 422. On a simulated company of 2{,}000 test-set employees, the API reports a significant gap for women ($-6.0$\pts, CI $-7.8$ to $-4.0$) and flags 52 potentially underpaid employees.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
% ======================================================================

\paragraph{What could invalidate the conclusions:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Unexplained is not proven.} Factors absent from the data (seniority, exact job title, region, firm size, performance) could legitimately explain part of the gaps.
\item \textbf{The reference is the population.} The model learns the average pay of a population that includes the discrimination we audit, and occupation can itself reflect discrimination: both bias the gaps towards zero.
\item \textbf{The data.} The census describes individuals, not companies; the target is total income rather than salary, binarised at a coarse \$50K threshold; and the data dates from 1994. The method transfers, the size of the gaps does not.
\item \textbf{Small groups.} Two race groups have fewer than 100 test people; their gaps cannot be interpreted. Calibration was checked globally; checking it within each group would further strengthen the audit.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Conclusion:} A calibrated reference model trained on job-related factors only is an effective screening tool for a pay-equity regulator. On this data, it reveals significant unexplained gaps for women ($-5.4$\pts) and Black employees ($-3.4$\pts), robust to the removal of the leaky capital columns, that a model trained on all columns would completely hide. We recommend using it to prioritise investigations, with the gap and its confidence interval as the trigger and the individual flags as the starting point for inspectors.
